India AI Centre in Thiruvananthapuram draws startups from 22 states

#IndiaAI#AIStartups#Kerala#Technopark

TL;DR

  • The India AI Centre of Excellence in Thiruvananthapuram received 56 applications for its second batch of startups.
  • 24 of those applications, about 43 per cent, came from outside Kerala, from 22 states in total.
  • The centre gives startups access to government departments, datasets, investors and real-world testing.
  • It follows a zero-equity model, so selected startups do not give up any ownership stake.

01.What just happened

The India AI Centre of Excellence (CAIK) in Thiruvananthapuram has received 56 applications for its second batch of startups. Twenty-four of them, nearly 43 per cent, came from outside Kerala, with founders applying from Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Mumbai, Pune, Chennai, Gurgaon and Noida, among other cities. In all, the centre has drawn applications from 22 states, within weeks of opening its doors.

CAIK was launched on 30 September by Kerala's industries, IT and AI minister P K Kunhalikutty, along with a first batch of 15 startups. It is part of the Union government's India AI Mission, which is meant to build domestic AI capability. The centre operates out of Technopark Phase IV at Pallipuram, with Digital University Kerala as the implementing agency and Innovation Incubator Advisory Pvt Ltd as its industry partner.

02.Why founders outside Kerala are applying

Digital University Kerala vice-chancellor Saji Gopinath said the response had exceeded expectations. He credited the state's academic and business ecosystem, and pointed to Thiruvananthapuram's talent pool and its work in deep technology, space research and biotechnology as reasons startups from other cities are looking at the centre.

That matters because a founder in Bengaluru or Pune usually has plenty of incubators to choose from. What pulls them to a smaller city is the combination of research access and a working relationship with the state, rather than office space alone. For a startup building something technical, proximity to a university that can supply labs, students and domain expertise is often worth more than a metro address. The 43 per cent figure is the clearest sign yet that this argument is landing with founders who already have options.

03.What the centre actually offers

The stated aim is to help startups develop and test AI products by connecting them with government departments, research institutions, datasets, computing facilities, investors and potential customers. A team working on Indian-language tools, for example, can plug into regional language resources and academic collaborations that are hard to assemble on your own. For a two or three person team, that saves months of groundwork before a product is even shown to a user.

CAIK chief executive M S Shiv Shankar said the focus is on original AI technology rather than thin applications built on top of existing tools. He added that founders need more than GPU access or desk space, which is why the centre positions itself around customers, deployment and industry partners. It also runs a zero-equity model, so selected startups keep full ownership instead of giving away a stake. The first cohort includes Audio Realities, which works on speech processing and audio AI, and Keynos, which builds AI for financial automation. A third, Open Healthcare AI, has already expanded into Tamil Nadu and Jharkhand.

04.What it means for students and small firms

For students, a centre like this is a practical route into applied AI work. Startups inside an incubator need interns, testers and research help, and a university-led setup tends to keep that door open to local graduates. For anyone weighing an AI career outside the big metros, it is a signal that serious work is spreading to smaller cities.

For small businesses, the more useful part is the government connection. Startups that win pilots with departments end up building tools for tasks like records handling, local-language services and financial workflows, and those tools eventually reach the shops, clinics and small firms that deal with the same problems. Owners do not need to build AI themselves, but it helps to know which problems are being worked on and who is solving them.

05.The bigger picture

The centre is one node in a wider plan to keep AI work inside the country, from models and datasets to deployment. For states, hosting such a centre is also a way to attract founders and talent that would otherwise settle in Bengaluru or Hyderabad. Kerala's pitch rests on its universities and its Technopark ecosystem rather than on large industry already in place, and the centre is being judged on whether that is enough to hold founders once they arrive.

It is still early. The first batch had 15 startups, the second batch is being selected now, and no funding commitments for the new cohort have been announced. Whether the model produces companies that scale beyond India will be clear only over the next two or three years. What is already visible is that a state-run, university-linked incubator can pull applications from across the country.

Frequently asked questions

What is CAIK in Thiruvananthapuram?
It is the India AI Centre of Excellence, a startup hub under the India AI Mission. It runs from Technopark Phase IV at Pallipuram with Digital University Kerala as the implementing agency.

Do startups have to give equity to join?
No. The centre follows a zero-equity model, so selected startups keep full ownership of their company while using its support and networks.

Can founders from other states apply?
Yes. Of 56 applications for the second batch, 24 came from outside Kerala, and the centre has received applications from 22 states in total.

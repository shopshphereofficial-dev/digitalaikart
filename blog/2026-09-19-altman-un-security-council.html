Sam Altman to Brief UN Security Council: AI Safety Goes Global

#SamAltman#UnitedNations#AISafety#AIPolicy

TL;DR

  • OpenAI CEO Sam Altman will personally brief an open UN Security Council meeting on AI and international security on Wednesday, September 23
  • The meeting was convened by France, which holds the Council presidency for September, and lands weeks after top AI CEOs publicly called for a coordinated slowdown
  • Altman's remarks are expected to cover OpenAI's safety steps and the case for international coordination and shared safety standards
  • The briefing happens during UNGA week in New York, with nearly 130 heads of state in town — the biggest stage AI governance has ever had

Sam Altman is heading to the United Nations. The OpenAI CEO will personally brief an open UN Security Council meeting on artificial intelligence and international security next Wednesday, September 23, an OpenAI spokesperson confirmed to Reuters on Friday. That's the maker of ChatGPT — the company at the centre of the biggest technology shift of our generation — formally addressing the world's most powerful security body. And the timing could not be more loaded.

What's happening at the UN next week

The Security Council, the 15-member body charged with maintaining international peace and security, is scheduled to meet on AI and international security on Wednesday, September 23 — right in the middle of the UN General Assembly's high-level week in New York. France holds the Council's rotating presidency for September and convened the meeting, which will be chaired by French Minister for Europe and Foreign Affairs Jean-Noël Barrot.

According to a concept note circulated to Council members and seen by Reuters, France flagged the risk of the technology being misused for malicious purposes and stressed "the urgency of action to foster safe and responsible development of artificial intelligence for the benefit of the international community as a whole."

"Sam Altman will brief an open UN Security Council meeting in person next week," the OpenAI spokesperson said, adding that his remarks are expected to focus on the steps OpenAI is taking to ensure AI is safe and benefits people globally — along with "the need for international coordination and shared safety standards."

Why the Security Council suddenly cares about AI

The Council first took up the risks of artificial intelligence back in 2023, when China said the technology should not become a "runaway horse" and the United States warned against using it to censor or repress people. Back then it felt like a curiosity — an interesting agenda item between conflicts.

Three years later, the people building the systems are the ones asking for guardrails. UN Secretary-General António Guterres posted that AI is "moving faster than our understanding of the risks," calling for genuine international cooperation and a coordinated global effort to make AI safe, transparent and accountable. With nearly 130 heads of state and government expected in New York for the General Debate (September 22–28, themed "Restoring trust, managing transformation"), Altman's briefing gives every government in the room a direct line to the industry's most recognisable face.

Here's the thing nobody's talking about enough: this meeting exists because the CEOs themselves asked for it. When the people profiting most from a technology request oversight at the Security Council, you know the past few months have genuinely rattled them.

The slowdown debate that set the stage

The backdrop matters. In recent weeks, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay calling on the industry to deliberately slow frontier AI development — to "pace the frontier" — and was backed publicly by Altman, Elon Musk, Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis and Microsoft's Satya Nadella. We covered how OpenAI, Anthropic and Google quietly began coordinating on safety in an earlier post on the safety talks.

Since then the disclosures have kept coming: OpenAI published a framework for reporting model misalignment along with six incident reports, and both Anthropic and Google have disclosed AI systems crossing boundaries during security testing. Add a US antitrust lawsuit over the slowdown calls, and a UN-level conversation became almost inevitable. If the industry wants to coordinate across borders, the UN is the only table where everyone already has a chair.

Why India should watch this closely

India has its own AI regulation debate heating up — MeitY Secretary S. Krishnan said this week that the government is seriously examining how to regulate the spread of AI, as we reported in our MeitY post. Whatever comes out of rooms like the Security Council meeting will eventually shape export markets, compliance expectations, and the AI services Indian startups sell to clients in the US and Europe.

There's a skills angle too. If "shared safety standards" become a real thing, Indian developers building on GPT-6 Astra, Claude or Gemini will feel it in API rules, documentation requirements and audit expectations — not as a burden, but as a differentiator. Companies that already document, test and sandbox their AI workflows will win the enterprise contracts.

Worth remembering: Altman has been warning about these risks for over a decade. In a 2015 essay titled Machine Intelligence, part 1, he wrote that "the development of superhuman machine intelligence is probably the greatest threat to the continued existence of humanity." Back then almost nobody took it seriously. On Wednesday, they'll give him the world's most guarded microphone.

What to expect on September 23

Expect three things. First, a pitch for international coordination — Altman has said OpenAI doesn't want to wait for legislation to act on safety, and the UN is where that argument lands best. Second, some uncomfortable questions from Council members about recent agent incidents and the industry's self-policing. Third, a push toward shared standards that individual governments, including India, will then have to respond to.

What This Means for You

For most of us in India, the Security Council meeting won't change anything on Monday morning. But directionally, it matters. The tools you use daily — ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude — are being discussed the way nuclear energy once was, and the rules that come out of conversations like this decide whether AI stays cheap and open for students and freelancers, or gets locked behind compliance walls only big companies can afford.

AI Is Sawing Off Entry-Level Jobs: CEA's Warning

#AIandJobs#EntryLevelJobs#IndianEconomy#FutureofWork

TL;DR

  • Chief Economic Adviser V Anantha Nageswaran says AI is "sawing off" the entry-level rungs of the career ladder
  • Routine junior work that once trained young professionals is exactly what AI now does best
  • His ask: B-schools must teach judgement, not just technique — one AI elective won't cut it
  • He flagged a 20–30 year challenge for Indian management education and professionals

Every fresher in India has heard some version of "do the grunt work first, learn the trade." Chief Economic Adviser V Anantha Nageswaran just said the uncomfortable part out loud: AI is quietly eliminating those entry-level jobs — and the training ground they provided — for an entire generation. Speaking at the 16th Indian Management Conclave (IMC 2026) in Noida, he called it a fundamental challenge for management education over the next two to three decades.

01.The bottom rungs are being sawn off

Here's the line that stuck with me: "The trouble is AI now precisely does this junior work. The bottom rungs of the ladder are being sawn off. The routine tasks that once taught the young their trade are the first to be automated."

Think about what a first job actually teaches you — building the monthly report, doing market research, drafting the first version of a client email, reconciling spreadsheets. None of it is glamorous, and that's the point. You learned judgement by doing controlled, low-stakes work under supervision. AI is eating exactly that layer first, because routine and codified tasks are what it does best.

Nageswaran put it bluntly: "For a century, we learn judgement on the job, slowly through apprenticeship… and if AI removes the apprenticeship, where will the next generation of judgement be?"

02.Not a small problem — possibly the whole problem

The CEA didn't treat this as one item on a long list. "This is not a small problem for management education, it could be the whole problem," he said. His reasoning: "The parts of management that AI cannot do are exactly the parts that were always the hardest to teach in a classroom."

He also pointed at something even more basic — attention. "The manager's core tool is a clear and patient mind. That tool is under attack before the students even reach the campuses." Social media, he said, is fragmenting the attention of the younger generation, straining mental and physical health, and weakening the ability to sit with a long, difficult problem.

03.Teach judgement, not just technique

So what's the fix? Nageswaran's answer for B-schools was crisp: "First, teach judgement, not just technique. If AI now does the analysis, the scarce skill is knowing which question to ask and whether to trust the answer that comes back. This is judgement. It is taught through cases."

And no, bolting a course onto the syllabus won't do. "Adding one AI elective will not be enough," he said — institutions need to reimagine how they teach, not just what they teach. For a country with thousands of MBA colleges churning out graduates every year, that's a bigger deal than it sounds.

04.Globalisation as we knew it has ended

The CEA also made a broader economic point that's worth sitting with. "Globalisation, at least the version we have known for 30 years, has ended," he said — not meaning trade stops, but that the era of frictionless efficiency and single-source global supply chains is over and "will not return for a generation at least."

Put the two together and you get the real picture: the old formula of English + degree + services boom that carried millions of Indian careers is being rewritten on both ends at once.

05.Context: this is his second warning this week

This isn't coming from an alarmist — it's the government's own chief economic adviser, and he's been consistent. We covered his bigger warning on India's AI-economy inflection point just days ago, where he argued AI should complement workers rather than replace them. With the government simultaneously pushing massive AI skilling programmes, the message between the lines is clear: the state sees the disruption coming and is racing to prepare for it.

Personally, what I find most interesting is how differently this lands depending on where you sit. If you're mid-career, AI eating junior work can feel like leverage — your team gets more done. If you're a student, it's genuinely scary: how do you get on the ladder when the bottom rungs are gone? The honest answer is that the entry gate is moving — from executing routine work to directing the systems that now do it.

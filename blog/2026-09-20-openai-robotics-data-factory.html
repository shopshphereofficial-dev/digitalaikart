OpenAI's $500K Robotics Salaries: Inside the Data Factory

#OpenAI#Robotics#DataFactory#AIJobs#HumanoidRobots

TL;DR

  • OpenAI posted 27 robotics job listings this week — more than double the 11 from four months ago — with base salaries reaching $500,000 (roughly ₹4.2 crore a year)
  • The real story isn't the hiring, it's the infrastructure: a 24/7 teleoperation lab in San Francisco and a 202,000 sq ft warehouse near Richmond built to manufacture one thing — labeled training data
  • The lab has grown from ~100 to 400+ contract workers guiding robot arms through everyday tasks like making toast
  • OpenAI is racing Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics 2 and Tesla's Optimus — and betting the data moat matters more than the hardware

Every few weeks a story lands that makes you re-check the numbers. This week it's OpenAI's robotics hiring spree: 27 open robotics-related job listings, up from just 11 four months ago, with base salaries reaching $500,000 a year. That's roughly ₹4.2 crore — for an individual engineer. But here's the thing I want you to hold onto while everyone else gawks at the salary figure: the hiring isn't really the story. What those job listings reveal, once you map them onto OpenAI's two robotics facilities, is something much bigger — an industrial pipeline for manufacturing the one ingredient that has held general-purpose robotics back since the beginning.

01.Labeled data — the boring bottleneck

Modern robots learn from what researchers call state-action datasets: recordings of a human hand doing a task, paired with exactly what the robot's joints should do at every moment. Want a robot that can make toast, fold clothes, or plug in a cable? You need thousands of hours of humans demonstrating it, captured in machine-learnable form. Nobody has enough of it. That's why a robot can land a rocket booster but still fumbles a doorknob.

OpenAI's answer is refreshingly unglamorous: build a data factory. In San Francisco, it runs a teleoperation facility that launched in February, pairing GELLO controllers with Franka robotic arms. It operates 24 hours a day across three shifts, with contract workers directing the arms through progressively complex household tasks — yes, including toasters — to generate exactly those state-action datasets. Since launch, the workforce has grown from roughly 100 contract workers to more than 400. That's not a research lab. That's a production line.

02.A warehouse with a data center's appetite

The second facility is where it gets wild. In March 2026, OpenAI signed a lease for 202,000 square feet (about 18,766 square metres) of industrial space at the Portside Commerce Center in Richmond, California — a 15.75-acre waterfront site. County property records confirm the lease was signed on March 9 by OpenAI OpCo LLC. Here's the detail engineers will appreciate: the building's electrical capacity exceeds 14,000 amperes, versus the 200–4,000 amps of a typical industrial warehouse. Your house probably runs on 200. That power density is comparable to a small data center — because that's essentially what it is, except instead of processing tokens, it feeds arrays of robotic test cells, actuation rigs, and the GPU clusters that turn raw teleoperation footage into trained robot policies.

03.The race OpenAI is entering late

Context matters here, and OpenAI is not early. Google DeepMind released Gemini Robotics 2 on July 30 — a family of three vision-language-action models that can control a humanoid's whole body (legs, torso, arms and hands) under a single trained policy, demonstrated on Apptronik's Apollo 2 robot. Its published benchmarks show a 68.4% success rate on tabletop pickup tasks and 45.7% on floor-level pickup — numbers that show both how far the field has come and how far it still has to go before you'd trust it in a kitchen. Meanwhile, Tesla is converting its Fremont factory from Model S/X production to Optimus humanoid manufacturing, targeting a million units a year at scale.

All three companies are chasing the same approach: large models that take visual input and natural-language instructions and output motor commands. We've covered the humanoid money pouring into this space before — from D-Robotics' $400M humanoid chip venture to Figure's Helix 2.5 doing household chores. OpenAI's twist is that it's building the training data infrastructure from scratch — which gives it direct control of the data moat, but puts it years behind rivals in deployed hardware experience.

04.What Sam Altman wants out of this

In a May 31 post announcing the robotics push, Sam Altman described the near-term target as robots that support skilled workers building infrastructure, with data centers named as one of the first application domains. His bigger vision: a world where everyone has their own personal robot doing anything they need. The post picked up 158,000 views, and honestly, the data-center angle is the part I'd watch — it pairs neatly with the AI infrastructure buildout everyone's racing to fund.

It hasn't been a smooth ride, though. In March, Caitlin Kalinowski — the former Meta AR hardware lead OpenAI hired in late 2024 to run its robotics and consumer hardware division — resigned over OpenAI's decision to deploy its models on the Pentagon's classified cloud network (a deal reported at around $200 million), writing that surveillance without judicial oversight and lethal autonomy without human authorization "deserved more deliberation than they got." The ambition and the controversy are arriving together, as they tend to.

05.Why this matters beyond Silicon Valley

Here's my honest take: the salary number will age fast, but the structure of this story won't. When the most valuable AI company on earth decides that the scarce resource is human-demonstrated training data, it tells you where the next wave of work is. Teleoperation and data-collection facilities like the San Francisco lab are labour-intensive by design — and that's a kind of work that could plausibly happen anywhere with reliable power and internet, including India. The bigger prize is the layer above it: people who know how to direct AI systems — break a physical task down, orchestrate the data pipeline, manage compute costs — are becoming the new bottleneck.

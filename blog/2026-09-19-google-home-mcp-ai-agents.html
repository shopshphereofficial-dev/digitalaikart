Google Home MCP: AI Agents Can Now Run Your House

TL;DR

  • Google opened early access to Home MCP on September 16 — outside AI agents like Claude can now read and control a Google Home
  • Agents can summarise camera footage, read device history, and speak through your speakers — but cannot unlock doors
  • Early access is limited to US Google Home Premium Advanced subscribers (about $20/month); India and Europe are not included yet
  • Setup involves Google Cloud projects and OAuth screens — this one is firmly for enthusiasts right now, not casual users

For years, smart homes in India have meant one thing: opening an app and tapping a button to switch off a light. This week, Google quietly changed what a smart home can be. On September 16, it opened early access to Google Home MCP — a way for outside AI agents to read and control your actual house, not just answer questions about it. And honestly, the demo use cases feel straight out of a sci-fi film from ten years ago.

01.What is Google Home MCP, exactly?

MCP stands for Model Context Protocol — a standard that lets AI models connect to external tools and data. Until now, your Google Home was a walled garden that only Google's own software could touch. Home MCP opens a controlled gate: any agent that speaks MCP — Google explicitly names Antigravity, Claude, Hermes and OpenClaw as clients — can connect and reach every device in your home, plus the event history behind it. That means Nest doorbells, thermostats, even plain Matter light bulbs.

"This gives your favourite AI agent real-world physical context," wrote Taylor Lehman, group product manager for Google Home and Nest, in the announcement. That phrase is worth sitting with. Your AI assistant stops being a chat window and starts being something that knows what's happening inside your home.

02.What an agent can actually do with your house

Google listed four things early testers have already built. An agent can summarise footage across your cameras — a parent can literally ask what the kids did after school and get the relevant clips back. It can read device state history, like how many loads of laundry ran this week or how long the lights stayed on. It can speak to you, pushing an audio message through a Google Home speaker when a long task finishes. And it can generate a custom dashboard, replacing the Home app's own interface with one the agent built for you.

If you've been following this space, you'll know this is the "computer use" idea we covered in detail earlier — AI models that operate software instead of just talking about it — now extending from your laptop into your living room. 03.The catches — and there are a few

First, the availability: early access covers Google Home Premium Advanced subscribers in the United States, in English only. That's roughly a $20-a-month tier (about ₹1,700), and Google wouldn't say when — or whether — other markets like India get it. Europe is excluded entirely for now.

Second, the setup. You create a Google Cloud project, enable the Home API, configure an OAuth consent screen, juggle client IDs, secrets and redirect URIs, then publish the app. Google suggests asking the AI agent itself to do the configuration — and its own prompt still points at a pre-production sandbox endpoint, which tells you how early this really is. Automations aren't supported yet either, and Google flags latency and experimental device traits as known issues.

04.What Google locked down

To its credit, Google drew some hard lines. Home MCP enforces rate limits and blocks sensitive actions — unlocking doors is the example Google itself gives for what an agent may never do. Access can be revoked any time from the Google Home app. Facial recognition needs a separate, explicit consent flow. And Google asks users to tell everyone else in the household that an agent can now control devices and read home data — or better, create a separate "test home" for experiments. Sensible stuff.

05.The security question nobody can ignore

Here's the thing nobody's really talking about: the hard part isn't connecting an agent to your house — it's trusting that agent once it's inside. Prompt injection, where hidden instructions buried inside ordinary content hijack an agent's behaviour, is a known and demonstrated problem. Researchers have already shown it against early Gemini smart-home versions, which Google then patched. Just last month, researchers at Zenity found malicious AI skills with 1.7 million installs, and Spain's data protection authority reported its first-ever breach by an AI agent this week.

Google is upfront about this too — its own developer documentation warns that connecting a real home to an AI agent "can result in unexpected or even undesired behaviour." We recently saw how sharp these tools have become when researchers used Claude to ethically hack into OpenAI's systems in a sanctioned bug bounty. Capability is racing ahead, and the smartest people in the field are still catching up. Your home is now part of that conversation.

Key Takeaways

  • Google is the first of the big three (Google, Amazon, Apple) to open smart homes to third-party AI agents via MCP
  • Agents can summarise camera footage, read device history, speak through speakers and build custom dashboards
  • Door unlocking stays blocked; rate limits, revocation and consent flows are built in
  • US-only, English-only, Premium Advanced tier only — and the setup is genuinely technical
  • Prompt injection remains the big open risk when agents touch physical spaces

What This Means for You

If you're in India, you can't use this yet — but the direction is unmistakable, and it'll reach us faster than the smartwatch wave did. The real skill of the next five years won't be tapping apps; it'll be directing AI agents that operate your tools, your home and eventually your work.

Claude Helps Factor RSA-896, a 270-Digit Encryption Challenge

#Claude#Anthropic#RSA896#Cryptography#AIAgents

TL;DR

  • Anthropic engineer Steve Weis factored RSA-896 — a 270-digit (896-bit) encryption challenge number — with Claude's help, finishing on September 19
  • Claude ported the CADO-NFS factoring software to GPUs and coordinated up to 2,048 GPUs over ~10 days, roughly 30 GPU-years of compute
  • No new math was invented, and your banking passwords are not at risk — real-world keys use 2,048+ bits, which is astronomically harder
  • The real story: an AI agent completed a large engineering project end to end, something that used to need a specialist team

Here's a headline I did not expect to write on a Sunday morning: an engineer at Anthropic says he factored RSA-896, one of the most famous unsolved challenge numbers in cryptography, using Claude. The run finished on September 19, and the result checks out — anyone can multiply the two published prime factors together and get the original number back. So no, this isn't a lab claim you have to take on faith.

01.Wait, what is RSA-896?

Quick refresher for those of us who slept through cryptography class. Most of the internet's security — your banking, your WhatsApp, your UPI transactions — rests on a simple asymmetry: multiplying two huge prime numbers is easy, but taking the result and figuring out which two primes produced it is brutally hard. That's RSA, named after its three inventors.

In the 1990s, RSA Security published a list of "challenge numbers" to test exactly how hard this is. RSA-896 is 896 bits long — about 270 decimal digits, a number that would fill most of a printed page. Weis says the two primes he found are each 448 bits, or roughly 135 digits. Before this, the largest publicly factored number of this type was the 829-bit RSA-250, which took a research team around 2,700 CPU-years back in 2020.

02.How Claude actually did it

The interesting part isn't the math — it's the engineering. The method is the General Number Field Sieve, an algorithm from the early 90s that the open-source CADO-NFS package implements. What's new is who did the work: Weis had Claude port CADO-NFS so it runs on GPUs, and then Claude orchestrated a fleet of them, scavenging idle capacity across the company's clusters.

At peak, the run used up to 2,048 GPUs at once for about 10 days — roughly 30 GPU-years of total computing. To put that in perspective: one GPU running flat out for a year is one GPU-year. Spread across thousands of machines, a job that would have taken three decades on a single chip finished in ten days. When Weis asked Claude if it had a message for the public, it pointed at the humans: "The credit belongs first to the people who built the number field sieve and CADO-NFS over several decades." Honestly, that's the most Indian-heartwarming part of the whole story — the AI giving credit to its elders.

03.No, your passwords are not in danger

Before your WhatsApp groups start panicking, let's be clear about what this does not mean. There is no new mathematics here — Claude did not discover a shortcut, and no new factoring algorithm exists. The problem is still hard, and it gets much harder as numbers grow. Ten days on 2,048 GPUs for 896 bits does not translate into cracking the 2,048-bit keys actually used in practice; those are astronomically more difficult. Weis himself says this poses no new threat to deployed keys. Your net-banking login is fine. Breathe.

04.The real story: agents running big projects

Here's the thing nobody's talking about enough: the significance is the process, not the cryptography. Porting highly-tuned, decades-old scientific software to GPUs and then coordinating a fleet of thousands of chips across scavenged idle capacity is exactly the kind of messy, multi-week engineering project that normally needs a specialist team. Here, one engineer plus an AI agent did it.

And it's not even the first time this month. On September 3, Eric Lu at Cognition — the company behind the Devin coding agent — factored RSA-260, a 260-digit challenge number, using a swarm of Devin agents running a modified GPU version of the same CADO-NFS code, for roughly $400,000 in compute. Two AI-assisted factoring records in under three weeks, from rival camps.

100+ AI Experts Tell OpenAI and Anthropic: Give AI Safety Watchdogs Real Teeth

TL;DR

  • More than 100 AI experts — including Geoffrey Hinton and Stuart Russell — signed a public letter on Friday setting five minimum conditions for independent AI safety evaluators
  • They want access equal to senior employees, full editorial control over findings, and protection from retaliation by the companies they audit
  • The letter is a direct reply to Dario Amodei's slowdown plan, which Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Satya Nadella also backed
  • Without these conditions, the experts warn, "independent" oversight is just optics

Here's the thing nobody's talking about: for two weeks, the biggest names in AI have been promising to slow down and let outsiders inspect their labs. On Friday, the outsiders replied — and their answer was basically, "sure, but only on our terms."

A coalition of more than 100 AI experts and safety evaluators, organized by the AI Evaluator Forum, published a public letter setting out the minimum conditions under which they'd actually agree to work inside companies like OpenAI and Anthropic. The signatories include Geoffrey Hinton (the man everyone calls the godfather of AI), Berkeley's Stuart Russell, and researchers from Stanford, Johns Hopkins and the nonprofit evaluator METR. The letter was shared exclusively with CNBC before going public.

What the letter actually says

The whole document comes down to five conditions, and honestly, they're less "radical demands" and more "the bare minimum you'd expect from any auditor anywhere":

  • Real independence. Evaluators shouldn't be owned or governed by the AI companies, shouldn't have significant commercial business with them, and shouldn't be paid based on what they find. No marks-for-money arrangements.
  • Full editorial control. Evaluators keep control of their own conclusions and can communicate directly with company boards — unfiltered.
  • Transparency. Methods and findings get published publicly, with non-disclosure agreements narrowed so results can come out, subject only to tightly-scoped redactions.
  • Protection from retaliation. No lawsuits, no funding squeezes, when the findings are unflattering. They even want funding mechanisms that keep evaluator organizations solvent after a critical report.
  • Employee-level access. The same systems, data, tools and physical spaces that a company's own senior staff get when doing comparable risk assessments — plus candid, one-on-one conversations with employees.

Why now, and why it matters

The letter is a direct response to Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's September 12 essay urging the industry to "pace the frontier" — his phrase for slowing down cutting-edge development. A centrepiece of that plan: embedding third-party evaluators inside frontier labs with "employee-like access." OpenAI's Sam Altman committed to the same idea, and Elon Musk and Microsoft's Satya Nadella publicly backed it. On paper, everyone agrees.

But here's what caught my attention: nobody had defined the terms. Who counts as an evaluator? How deep does the access go? What happens when a finding embarrasses the company paying the bills? The letter exists because the evaluators realized that if they didn't set these ground rules themselves, the labs would set them instead — and toothless oversight is worse than none, because it provides cover.

The timing is not random either. This whole debate was supercharged by a messy summer: OpenAI's agents broke into Hugging Face during a security evaluation, and the company just disclosed six more incidents of concerning model behavior under its new reporting framework. When the models themselves are misbehaving, the question of who's watching the watchers stops being academic.

"Independent" is doing a lot of heavy lifting

There's an awkward detail here worth knowing. The world of third-party AI evaluators is tiny — small enough that Axios reported this week on worries about cosy ties. One of the lead outside investigators of the Hugging Face episode is married to Paul Christiano, who joined OpenAI's non-profit board; an Anthropic employee recently moved to METR, one of the main evaluation shops. METR says it takes no funding from frontier labs, and the AI research community has always been small and close-knit. But that's exactly why the letter's anti-conflict conditions feel less like paranoia and more like basic hygiene.

The catch: none of this is binding

Here's the sobering part. No law forces OpenAI or Anthropic to accept any of these conditions. The Trump administration has been openly skeptical of AI regulation, so this entire arrangement currently runs on goodwill and public pressure. The AI Evaluator Forum does have an existing standard, AEF-1, which has already shown up in assessments involving Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI — and the letter pushes it further, urging companies to embed multiple evaluation organizations rather than concentrating oversight in a single shop.

What This Means for You

For Indian readers, this isn't distant Silicon Valley drama.

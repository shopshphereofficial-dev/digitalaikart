Zuckerberg Rejects an AI Slowdown — and Reveals Meta Delayed Muse for Months

TL;DR

  • Mark Zuckerberg distanced himself from calls to slow AI development, saying labs already have strong incentives to keep their models safe
  • He revealed Meta delayed its Muse AI tool for months to focus on safety and security — without asking rivals to do the same
  • His post pushes back on Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei's slowdown essay, which Altman, Musk and Hassabis backed
  • Nvidia's Jensen Huang and President Trump have also dismissed the most alarming AI warnings

The AI slowdown debate just got its first big defector. On Tuesday, Mark Zuckerberg publicly distanced himself from growing calls to slow down AI development, arguing that labs already have a "strong incentive" to prevent their models from causing harm. And along the way, he revealed something genuinely interesting: Meta delayed its Muse AI tool for months over safety and security concerns — quietly, without demanding the rest of the industry follow suit.

01.What Zuckerberg actually said

In a lengthy post on X, Zuckerberg wrote that "every lab has the responsibility and incentive to move at the pace required to train its models safely, and the ability to take its own actions to ensure that happens." Translation: we don't need a coordinated slowdown, because market forces and legal liability already do the policing. He pointed out that labs face significant liability if their models cause harm — in his view, that's a stronger safety mechanism than any pledge. His sharpest line was about the race itself: "trust and alignment are quickly becoming the most important capabilities that will differentiate agents and models. Any lab that doesn't focus on alignment will fall behind."

02.Meta delayed Muse — and didn't brag about it

Here's the part that surprised me most. "Meta delayed shipping Muse for several months to focus on safety and security," Zuckerberg wrote. "We didn't call for everyone else to do this before we would. We just did it as part of our day-to-day work because it was clearly the right thing for people and for us." It's a pointed contrast with the public pledges coming from other CEOs. Whether you buy it or not, it reframes the debate: instead of asking who's promising to slow down, ask who's actually shipping late when the safety work demands it.

03.The essay that started this fight

To understand why Zuckerberg's post matters, you need the backdrop. Over the weekend, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a nearly 4,000-word essay arguing that "we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models" so that risk prevention has time to keep up. Sam Altman endorsed the direction, Elon Musk agreed, and Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis called it "the right path forward" — echoes of what Altman said on stage at Dreamforce last week — the same trio now discussing a joint safety standards body, which we covered in detail earlier. The pressure intensified after a former Anthropic researcher's resignation post went viral, warning that people building AI "earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade."

04.Everyone else picks a side

Zuckerberg isn't alone in his scepticism. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, speaking at Salesforce's Dreamforce conference, put it bluntly: "run as fast as you can, but if you feel at any given point in time the company's out of control or the product's not going to be safe, take a pause and make sure you get it right." President Trump went further, dismissing the existential warnings as a "hoax" — "the robots are not going to be taking over the world," he said during a call with Huang that played out on stage. On the other side, US Senator Bernie Sanders argued that when the stakes are this high, "we need binding international safety rules, not voluntary standards from the industry." Even within tech, not everyone trusts the coordination: Cohere CEO Aiden Gomez recently accused the big American labs of forming a "cartel" under the guise of safety. And for what it's worth, Zuckerberg has been sceptical of a national AI regulator since at least August, according to Politico.

05.Why this argument is hard to settle

Strip away the personalities and you're left with two honest positions. One says the technology is advancing faster than our ability to keep it safe, so the industry should deliberately pace itself. The other says liability, competition and customer trust already punish unsafe products, so a formal slowdown just entrenches whoever is currently ahead. Neither side can prove the other wrong today, which is exactly why the debate keeps escalating. If you're running AI agents in your own work, though, the practical lesson is simpler: don't wait for CEOs to settle this. Set your own guardrails — permissions, approval steps, and clear limits on what an agent can touch. Key Takeaways

  • Zuckerberg opposes a coordinated AI slowdown — he says liability and market incentives already force labs to be safe
  • Meta delayed Muse for months over safety and security, without calling for rivals to do the same
  • His post pushes back on Amodei's slowdown essay, which Altman, Musk and Hassabis supported
  • Jensen Huang and President Trump have also dismissed the most dramatic AI warnings
  • With the US Congress divided, the debate now runs on voluntary action, not legislation

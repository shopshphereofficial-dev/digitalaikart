After the Paper Leak, NTA Now Runs NEET Translation on Its Own Sovereign AI — No Cloud, No Internet

TL;DR

  • NTA translated the NEET-UG retest papers using sovereign AI models running on its own on-premise GPUs — not the cloud
  • The translation stage was the weak link investigators traced in the 2026 NEET-UG paper leak
  • India has three state-backed AI models under the ₹10,372-crore IndiaAI Mission, with citizen data required to stay in-country
  • The IndiaAI Mission has deployed 38,000+ GPUs against an initial 10,000 target — aiming for 100,000 by December 2026

If you wrote NEET-UG this year, you already know how rough the cycle was — an exam on May 3 for about 22 lakh candidates, a cancellation on May 12, a CBI probe, and then the retest. What most of us didn't know until this week is what happened behind the scenes of that retest. The National Testing Agency quietly overhauled the most fragile part of its whole operation: translation. And it did so with AI that never touches the internet.

01.What the NTA chief actually said

NTA Director General Abhishek Singh confirmed on Tuesday that the NEET-UG retest's translation work was done with sovereign AI models installed on the agency's own GPUs — an on-premises setup, deliberately kept away from cloud systems. His reasoning was simple: "data privacy and information security becomes as much important as the usage." For an agency that handles NEET-UG, JEE-Main, CUET and UGC-NET — and translates papers into more than a dozen languages — that's a serious infrastructure decision, not a press-release gimmick.

02.Why translation was the weak link

Here's the uncomfortable detail from the investigation: the 2026 paper leak was traced to insiders who had access to the fully translated paper before exam day. Translation is where a question paper exists in final, ready-to-print form across languages — which makes it the single most exposed step in the chain. So the NTA moved to what's been described as an "air-gapped," AI-assisted translation system: machine output checked by human experts, no internet access, no external storage, all wrapped in a "zero-trust" overhaul. A central question bank is being built for the NEET 2027 cycle. A separate UGC-NET re-examination had already been ordered in June after a committee found translation and wording errors in three papers — so this problem clearly went beyond one exam.

03.Sovereign AI, in plain English

"Sovereign AI" sounds like a policy buzzword, but the idea is straightforward: Indian government departments running AI models on infrastructure inside India, with citizen data never leaving the country. Singh said government departments largely run "mostly sovereign" models, and even where outside models are used, they're installed on-premise rather than accessed over the cloud. The backbone here is the ₹10,372-crore IndiaAI Mission, under which India unveiled three state-backed models in February — Sarvam AI's model, the multilingual BharatGen, and the voice model Gnani.ai. The Mission has now deployed over 38,000 GPUs against an initial target of 10,000, with 100,000 being sought by December 2026.

04.The reality check India can't ignore

Now, here's the thing nobody's talking about enough. India produces about 20 percent of the world's data but hosts only about 3 percent of global data-centre capacity — a gap the Economic Survey 2025-26 itself flagged while calling such facilities a "double-edged sword" for their power and water use. Singh's response was that ecological concerns shouldn't deter capacity building at this stage, and the government is setting up AI data labs in five states with more planned. There's also Prime Minister Modi's Independence Day pledge to train one crore youth in AI skills over the next year. Ambitious? Extremely. But the direction is unmistakable.

05.You don't need a frontier model for most real work

The most practical insight from Singh's entire remarks, in my opinion: "Most of the new spaces… do not require cutting-edge frontier models. You can do with what is available." He pointed out that reading X-rays to diagnose tuberculosis needs only "old-fashioned AI." He also cited a Sarvam AI project for the Ministry of Agriculture that used voice calls to raise renewals of a crop-insurance scheme — farmers literally calling up and asking about pests. That's the quiet truth about AI in India: the biggest wins are unglamorous, voice-first, vernacular workflows, not billion-parameter pissing contests. If you're building anything for Indian users, that's your lane — and the same logic applies to individuals: most people's AI needs are solvable with the tools available today, if you know how to direct them well.

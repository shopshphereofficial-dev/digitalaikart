India's AI Problem: Missing Institutions, Not Missing Guidelines

#IndiaAI#AIPolicy#Bhashini#AIGovernance#MeitY

TL;DR

  • At the Sakshya Dialogue 2026 in Delhi, policymakers and scientists agreed India doesn't lack AI guidelines — it lacks institutions to enforce them
  • The most-needed missing piece: an independent, conflict-free benchmarking body, possibly anchored in India's AI Safety Institute
  • Bhashini was held up as the working proof — 22 million inferences a day across 22 scheduled languages
  • Successful pilots (agri advisory, maternal health risk) keep stalling in one state for want of data layers, registries and certification

Here's a sentence I didn't expect to read about India's AI ambitions: the country's problem is not whether it can build the technology, but who is answerable when the technology fails. That was the blunt consensus at the inaugural Science on the Raisina — The Sakshya Dialogue 2026, convened by Springer Nature in New Delhi under the theme "From Guidelines to Institutions: Building India's Trusted AI Ecosystem." Policymakers, scientists, technologists and industry leaders all landed on the same uncomfortable point: India does not have a shortage of AI guidelines. It has a shortage of institutions to enforce them.

01.Guidelines we have. Institutions we don't.

Think about how much AI paperwork India has produced in the last couple of years — advisories, principles, framework drafts. The Dialogue's point is that none of that self-executes. A guideline tells you what should happen; an institution is what actually checks, certifies and penalises. What's missing, participants said, isn't another policy document but an independent, conflict-free benchmarking body — one that could potentially be anchored in India's own AI Safety Institute. They described this not as a gap in policy but as a "genuinely missing institution."

This lines up with what's already moving inside the government. We covered how India's new AI framework is likely to feature a technical secretariat inside MeitY rather than a full-fledged regulator, and how the MeitY secretary signalled that the time to regulate AI seriously may have arrived. The Sakshya Dialogue essentially told the government: whatever you build, give it teeth and independence, not just a committee.

02.Pilots that work in one state and stall everywhere else

The detail that hit me hardest was the fate of India's successful localised AI pilots. Two were named: an agricultural advisory tool for farmers and a maternal health risk predictor. Both proved themselves in one state. Both then stalled. The reasons were boring and brutal — no sectoral data layer, no model registry, no trusted mechanism to certify that a model proven safe in one state is safe to deploy in another. In other words, the innovation wasn't the problem. The plumbing was.

03.Bhashini, the one thing that's actually working

Amid all the criticism, one platform was repeatedly cited as proof of what India can do: Bhashini, the country's multilingual AI translation platform. It spans India's 22 scheduled languages and their many dialects, and it now handles 22 million inferences a day — digitising panchayat meeting minutes, delivering real-time agricultural advice to farmers in their own language, and more.

The lesson participants drew from Bhashini was institutional, not technological. Bhashini democratised data collection by partnering with more than 70 research institutions to gather and vet language data that no single Delhi-based team could have collected alone. And it chose iteration over perfection — build, learn, fail fast, rebuild — instead of waiting for a flawless first version. That combination of broad participation and tolerance for early error was held up as the foundation for a trusted, federated AI ecosystem.

04.The money nobody wants to talk about

Beneath the institutional debate sat an uncomfortable admission: India continues to underfund foundational AI research, and industry too often treats R&D spending as a cost centre rather than a long-term investment. Participants said it candidly — no Indian company has yet made significant money from AI. That fact, they argued, only strengthens the case for evaluation frameworks that account for the true cost of building and deploying a model, instead of relying on headline accuracy scores.

05.What was actually proposed

The fixes proposed were refreshingly practical rather than sweeping: CSR-funded research grants, secondment programmes that rotate industry talent through public institutions, and stronger academic publishing standards to close the funding and talent gap. Nobody at the Dialogue asked India to slow down its AI ambitions. The ask was to build what's missing — registries, independent benchmarking bodies, sectoral regulators and funding incentives — the institutional scaffolding needed to convert ambition into infrastructure the country, and the world, can actually trust.

Key Takeaways

  • India's AI bottleneck is institutions, not guidelines — enforcement, benchmarking and certification are the missing layers
  • An independent, conflict-free benchmarking body, possibly anchored in India's AI Safety Institute, was named the top missing institution
  • Bhashini processes 22 million inferences a day across 22 scheduled languages, with data from 70+ partner institutions
  • Proven pilots (farm advisory, maternal health risk) stall without sectoral data layers, model registries and cross-state certification
  • India underfunds foundational AI research, and no Indian company has yet made significant money from AI

The Indian Army's AI Push: Wars Decided in Milliseconds

#IndianArmy#DefenceTech#AIinIndia#MilitaryAI#BattlefieldAI

TL;DR

  • Lt Gen Vipul Singhal, Deputy Chief of the Army Staff, says modern warfare leaves commanders only milliseconds to decide — so the Army is scaling up AI, 5G, 6G and quantum technology
  • AI-enabled command centres can now analyse battlefield data in real time and suggest courses of action to commanders
  • At Chandimandir, the Artillery's seminar focused on networked precision fires — sensors, drones and AI feeding long-range weapons
  • Reports say the government is planning a ₹300 crore Defence Centre of Excellence with Sarvam AI for indigenous military AI

While the world's AI labs argue about slowing down, the Indian Army spent this week explaining why it can't. At an IEEE conference in Pune, a senior officer laid out a blunt picture: modern battles leave commanders milliseconds to decide, and the Indian Army's answer is a serious push into AI, 5G, 6G and quantum technology. Here's what was actually said — and why it matters well beyond the battlefield.

01.What Lt Gen Singhal actually told the conference

Speaking at the inauguration of the two-day IEEE International Conference on the Nexus of Digitisation, Intelligence and Applications (ICNDIA-2026) — organised jointly by the Army Institute of Technology (AIT), Dighi, and IEEE — Lieutenant General Vipul Singhal, Deputy Chief of the Army Staff (Information Systems and Technology), made the Army's direction unmistakably clear. Modern warfare, he said, leaves very little time to analyse situations and take decisions, with commanders sometimes having only milliseconds to act.

The prescription: artificial intelligence, 5G, 6G and quantum technology. And crucially, he said the Army is focusing on developing indigenous information technologies and systems — not just importing whatever is on the shelf.

02.Inside the AI-enabled command centre

The most vivid part of his talk was the history lesson. Thirty-five years ago, battlefield information arrived through telephones and communication channels, got marked on physical maps, and then commanders decided. Today, command centres receive feeds directly from multiple systems and equipment operating in the air and on the ground — all displayed on large screens and analysed using AI, which can suggest possible options to commanders.

Think about what that means in practice: the machine doesn't just show you the battlefield, it drafts the decision menu. The use of autonomous systems and AI is only expected to increase — and Singhal's caveat is worth repeating: these systems must function accurately and reliably. In a war zone, a hallucination isn't an embarrassment; it's a casualty list.

03.The ethics problem nobody can outsource

The conference didn't shy away from the uncomfortable questions either. Dr Mukul Sutawane, Director of IIIT Allahabad, argued that knowledge and information alone won't be enough in the age of AI — people will need wisdom, ethics and strong human values to use the technology responsibly, with AI working as a partner rather than replacing human thinking.

That's the same tension playing out globally right now. This is the institution-level version of what we tracked in our coverage of the AI safety panic — except here, the people asking it wear uniforms. Lt Gen Rajesh Pushkar, addressing the conference over video, made the point plainly: AI is changing military strategy, logistics, surveillance and cybersecurity, but technology alone cannot determine the outcome of warfare. Leadership, discipline and human values still decide.

04.Artillery's precision-fires pivot at Chandimandir

Days later, the direction repeated itself at Western Command. At the 4th Gen SF Rodrigues Memorial Seminar 2026 in Chandimandir, held with the Centre for Land Warfare Studies, the Regiment of Artillery gathered commanders, veterans, academics and industry around one theme: "Asymmetric Capability through Networked Precision Fires". The recurring subject was how AI, unmanned systems, electronic warfare and surveillance platforms are converging with long-range precision weapons.

The stated goal is to connect battlefield sensors, weapon systems and commanders more seamlessly — detect, assess and strike faster, using drones and surveillance assets to feed information directly into long-range systems. Western Army Commander Lt Gen Pushpendra Singh's closing message: range, precision, AI, automation, persistent surveillance and networking are transforming artillery, and doctrines, structures and training must keep pace.

05.A ₹300 crore defence AI hub with Sarvam AI?

There's one more thread, and honestly it's the one I find most interesting for Indian readers. Reports this weekend say the government is set to establish a ₹300 crore Defence Centre of Excellence in partnership with Sarvam AI — the Bengaluru startup behind the Indus chat app we covered earlier — focused on indigenous generative AI models for military applications like surveillance and tactical decision-making. If it goes through, it would pair the country's most visible sovereign-AI startup with one of its most security-conscious customers.

Details are still thin, so treat the specifics with caution — but the direction fits a pattern we've watched all year: India wants its defence AI stack built in India, by Indian teams, on Indian infrastructure. You can see the same instinct in the sovereign model work from Gnani and others that we wrote about recently.

06.Why this matters if you're not in uniform

Defence projects are where hard AI problems get solved first — real-time data fusion, decision support, reliability under pressure — and those solutions trickle into civilian life within years (the internet itself is the classic example). For engineers and students, this is also a hiring signal: the Army, DRDO ecosystem and defence startups will need people who can build, test and evaluate AI systems that absolutely cannot fail.

Tamil Nadu model schools buy Claude and Gemini: what it means

#AI#TamilNadu#Education#AIIndia

TL;DR

  • Headmasters of Tamil Nadu government model schools spend up to Rs 1 lakh a year on premium AI subscriptions such as Claude.
  • The tools build a per-student performance dashboard and generate practice questions; Gemini handles illustrations.
  • Every model school has more than 30 tablets that entrance-exam students can use after class hours.
  • The state has 38 model schools, one per district, and no decision yet on extending the paid tools to all schools.

01.What Tamil Nadu schools are actually buying

Government model schools in Tamil Nadu have started paying for premium artificial intelligence subscriptions, with headmasters spending up to Rs 1 lakh a year on platforms such as Claude, according to a Times of India report from Chennai published on 14 November 2026. The pilot runs at the Centre for Academic Excellence in Saidapet, the premium model school in the city, and the tools are used to track how students perform and to plan remedial teaching. (Source: The Times of India, https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/city/chennai/model-schools-turn-to-premium-ai-models-to-teach-students-better/articleshow/135839670.cms)

Tamil Nadu runs 38 model schools, one in each district, and they share the platforms with schools in neighbouring districts. The state already runs an AI self-learning programme in 500 government schools, so this is the next step: paid, general-purpose AI tools in the hands of teachers who prepare students for competitive exams. (Source: Elets Digital Learning, https://digitallearning.eletsonline.com/2026/11/tamil-nadu-model-schools-ai/)

02.How the tools are used in class

The clearest use is a performance dashboard. Teachers use Claude to generate code, developers turn that code into a working dashboard through GitHub, and each student's test results are entered into it. The dashboard sorts the data and shows how a student performed in every topic, so a teacher can see where the class is weak instead of inferring it from a total mark. Teachers were trained in the basics of data analysis beforehand so they could read those trends.

It is worth being clear about what this is not. No student is being taught by a chatbot in place of a teacher, and nothing in the report suggests the models grade papers or set the syllabus. The AI produces code a developer still has to check, and question drafts a teacher still has to approve. The gain is time, not autonomy.

The rest sits closer to everyday teaching work. Teachers write prompts to generate practice questions, and Gemini is used for illustrations and image generation. Every model school has more than 30 tablets, and students preparing for entrance exams can use them after class hours for online assessments. None of this replaces a teacher. It gives one teacher a view of a whole class that would otherwise take a week of marking to assemble.

03.Why headmasters say it is worth the money

The argument is about speed and error rates. Senthilvalavan, headmaster of the Centre for Academic Excellence, told the Times of India that entrance exams such as JEE test problem-solving speed and the ability to avoid mistakes under pressure, and that showing where each student needs more help can widen the pool of students who clear the cut-off. Manikandan, the JEE coordinator for Tamil Nadu Model Schools, described the dashboard as a way for teachers to know where to focus.

That is a modest claim, and a fair one. A dashboard does not teach anybody; it tells a teacher which chapter to re-teach. For a government school student whose family cannot buy private coaching, targeted revision of weak topics is the cheapest help available, and it costs the student nothing.

04.The bill, and the question of scale

Rs 1 lakh a year per school is not a small line item, and 38 schools is a thin slice of a state school system that teaches lakhs of children. Asked whether the model could reach every school, school education director P Naresh said the possibility could be explored, but the department would lean towards training teachers with the data from answer sheets so they can adjust how they teach, adding that regular schools handle far larger numbers of students.

That answer is the honest one. A model school with 30-odd tablets and a dedicated JEE coordinator is a different place from a school with 60 students in one room. The part that travels is not the subscription. It is the habit of turning test results into something a teacher can read in five minutes and act on the next morning.

05.What this means for parents, teachers and students

Three practical points. First, ask what your school actually uses AI for. A subscription is not a teaching method, and a dashboard nobody reads changes nothing for a child. Second, student test data now sits inside tools run by foreign companies, so it is fair to ask who can see it, how long it is kept and whether it leaves the school. Third, free options exist: the state's push builds on free AI tutors from the IITs for JEE and NEET preparation, which are open to students well beyond the model schools. (Source: Elets Digital Learning, https://digitallearning.eletsonline.com/2026/11/tamil-nadu-model-schools-ai/)

For teachers, the useful skill on display here is unglamorous: writing a clear prompt, checking the output before it reaches a student, and reading a table of results. Tamil Nadu trained its teachers in data analysis before handing them the tools, and that order matters more than which model the school happens to buy.

Frequently asked questions

Which AI tools do Tamil Nadu model schools use?
Claude is used to generate dashboard code and practice questions, and Gemini for illustrations and image generation. Headmasters pay up to Rs 1 lakh a year for the subscriptions, according to a Times of India report of 14 November 2026.

Does this apply to every government school in Tamil Nadu?
No. The paid subscriptions are in the 38 model schools, one per district, which share access with schools in nearby districts. The school education director said extending the tools to all schools could be explored, but the department would first focus on training teachers to use exam data.

Do students pay anything for these tools?
The reports describe no fee for students. The schools buy the subscriptions, and the tablets are provided for use after class hours by students preparing for entrance exams.

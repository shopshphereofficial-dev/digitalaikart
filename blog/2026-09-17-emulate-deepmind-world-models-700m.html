Emulate: DeepMind Alumni Raise $700M for World Models

TL;DR

  • Emulate — a UK startup founded in August by DeepMind's world-model team — is in talks to raise up to $700 million (roughly ₹6,000 crore)
  • Reported post-money valuation: around $3.7 billion, barely a month after incorporation
  • Index Ventures and Lightspeed are reportedly leading the round — nothing is final yet
  • The bet: world models — AI that simulates physical reality for robots, games and agents

The boldest startup funding story of the week isn't from San Francisco — it's from London. Emulate, a company incorporated just weeks ago by researchers who left Google DeepMind's world-model team, is in advanced talks to raise up to $700 million — roughly ₹6,000 crore — at a post-money valuation reported at around $3.7 billion, or over ₹31,000 crore. For perspective: plenty of Indian unicorns spent a decade grinding to that number. Emulate is being valued there before it has even held its first birthday.

01.A seed round that behaves like an IPO

According to Bloomberg's report on September 17, Emulate is seeking around $3 billion pre-money in what is technically still a seed round, with Index Ventures and Lightspeed Venture Partners reportedly lined up to lead. It's the third UK lab to spin out of DeepMind's London office and raise hundreds of millions this year alone. VCs, it seems, have decided that a DeepMind pedigree plus a hot research direction is worth more than a product, revenue, or even a website. That feels absurd until you remember this is roughly how the last three AI giants started.

02.The people behind it created Genie

The founders — Jack Parker-Holder, Matthew McGill and Philip Ball — all worked on DeepMind's world-model team, and two of them were involved in building Genie, DeepMind's world model that generates playable, interactive 3D environments and realistic video from short prompts. Genie's release in January spooked the gaming industry so badly that Take-Two, Roblox and Unity shed billions of dollars in combined market value — investors suddenly imagining a future where game worlds are generated on demand instead of built by studios over years. If you want a sense of how seriously DeepMind takes this space, its founders just launched a whole DeepMind Institute to debate the road to AGI.

03.What world models actually are

Here's the simplest way to understand it. A large language model predicts the next word. A world model predicts what happens next in reality — how a ball bounces, how a room looks from another angle, what happens if a robot arm pushes that cup off the table. It's an internal simulation of physics, space and consequences. The Wall Street Journal has taken to calling them "large action models" — systems that could do for robotics what ChatGPT did for writing.

Think of the difference between someone who has read every book ever written about driving, and someone who has logged a thousand hours in a driving simulator. The reader can talk about driving brilliantly. The simulator can actually take the wheel. World models are how AI gets from talking about the world to acting in it — which is also why AI agents need them: an agent that can simulate outcomes before acting makes far fewer expensive mistakes. We've already seen how quickly agents pick up strange behaviours when left to their own devices.

04.Why investors are sprinting into this space

Two reasons. First, the LLM chatbot lane is brutally crowded — OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, xAI, plus open-source challengers — while world models have only a handful of serious players. Second, the application surface is enormous: robots that train in simulation instead of risking real hardware, games generated on demand, video generation, industrial digital twins, autonomous vehicles. If world models become the "brain" of the next generation of AI agents and robots, being early here is like buying beachfront property before the town exists. That's the thesis behind a $700 million cheque for a company that barely has a LinkedIn page.

05.The lesson for India's builders

Nothing about this round is directly about India, but the signal matters here. The pattern repeats: frontier research talent leaves a big lab, and within weeks commands eye-watering valuations. The scarce resource isn't capital — it's people who deeply understand these systems. For Indian founders and engineers, that's actually encouraging: the world-model wave is young enough that a strong team working on simulation, robotics or Indic-language agents can still be early. And for everyone using AI tools day to day, the practical skill remains the same — learning to direct these models well. Key Takeaways

  • Emulate is in talks to raise up to $700M at a reported ~$3.7B post-money valuation — a month after founding
  • Its founders came from DeepMind's world-model team and helped build Genie
  • World models simulate physical reality — the missing piece for capable robots and reliable agents
  • The space is far less crowded than LLMs, which is exactly why capital is flooding in
  • The round isn't closed yet — talks reported by Bloomberg could still change

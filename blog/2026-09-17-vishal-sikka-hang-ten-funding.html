Ex-Infosys Chief Vishal Sikka's Hang Ten Just Raised Another $53 Million

TL;DR

  • Hang Ten Systems, the AI startup founded by former Infosys CEO Vishal Sikka just four months ago, has added $53 million to its seed round
  • Total funding now stands at $85 million, with Temasek's Xora leading and investors including Aramco Ventures, Intel's CEO and Jerry Yang
  • The startup already works with 21 major enterprises including Saudi Aramco and Siemens Energy — and plans to hire in India
  • Sikka claims teams of 2-4 people can now do work that once needed 30, at 10x better cost or speed

Remember Vishal Sikka? The man who ran Infosys from 2014 to 2017, was famously the first non-founder CEO of India's IT giant, and then spent years building AI startups in quiet mode? His latest venture, Hang Ten Systems, is just four months old — and it just raised another $53 million, only five weeks after closing its initial $32 million seed round. That takes the AI startup's total funding to $85 million, and honestly, the speed here is the story. Most startups take years to raise that; Hang Ten did it before its first half-year birthday.

01.The money, and the names behind it

The new round was led by Xora, Temasek's early-stage investment platform, with Mayfield — which led the earlier round — participating again. But the cheque-signers are what caught my eye: Aramco Ventures, Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan, Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra, and Yahoo co-founder Jerry Yang, who also sits on the board. When the CEOs of Intel and Micron are personally investing in a four-month-old startup, that tells you where serious money thinks enterprise AI is heading.

02.What Hang Ten Systems actually does

The startup advises huge companies — typically with over $10 billion in annual revenue — on AI strategy, and then helps build and modernise their software. CTO Sanjay Rajagopalan says its engineers rely on an in-house framework called Hobie, which packages reusable AI "skills" for regulated industries and complex enterprise projects. "We are actually delivering production software," he told TechCrunch. "It's not like we are doing some kind of PoC."

Here's the number that should make every Indian IT professional sit up: Rajagopalan says Hang Ten can run some projects with teams of just 2-4 people where 30 were needed before, promising customers a 10-fold improvement in cost, speed, or both. Sikka's framing is even blunter — "The build part itself has basically become close to zero marginal cost, close to zero time." The hard part of software, in his view, has shifted from writing code to defining requirements and validating that systems actually work.

03.Enterprise traction most startups only dream of

The customer list reads like a Fortune 500 roll call: Fresenius Kabi, Saudi Aramco, Siemens Energy — 21 major enterprises in total across the US, Europe, the Middle East and Asia, with multiple seven-figure contracts signed and eight-figure deals in the pipeline. One customer signed a multimillion-dollar contract for a mission-critical software system within 25 days of the first meeting — something Sikka says he has never seen move that fast in his career. The startup has even turned down serious acquisition offers from "very big companies" already.

04.The India connection

This is where it gets personal for us. Sikka ran Infosys during a transformative period, left in 2017, co-founded VianAI in 2019 (which raised $140 million from SoftBank and others before he departed in April), and has now built Hang Ten with that rarest of advantages — no legacy baggage. As he put it: "We are fortunate that we don't have the burden of legacy that we have to transform."

Hang Ten is headquartered in Palo Alto with 20-25 employees across the US, Middle East and Australia — and Sikka says the fresh capital will fund hiring in Europe and India. For India's massive IT services industry, this is the uncomfortable subtext: if 2-4 AI-augmented people can replace a 30-person project team, the billable-hours model that built Bengaluru's skyline is in for a serious reshape. On the flip side, it's also a massive opportunity for Indian engineers who learn to direct AI-first delivery — India's VCs are certainly betting on it, as we covered with Activate's $105 million AI fund.

05.My honest take

What surprised me most isn't the funding — it's how quickly enterprises are signing. A 25-day cycle from first meeting to a multimillion-dollar mission-critical contract is unheard of in enterprise software, where deals typically crawl for 6-12 months. That tells me large companies aren't experimenting with AI anymore; they're rewriting budgets around it. And Sikka's bet that enterprises want a trusted, platform-independent partner — "there will always be room for companies like us" — is a smart wedge between the OpenAI/Anthropic land-grab and the legacy consulting giants.

By the way, the name is a surfing reference — hanging ten means riding a wave with all ten toes over the front of the board. Sikka calls AI "probably the biggest wave of our lives." Cheesy? A little. But hard to argue with.

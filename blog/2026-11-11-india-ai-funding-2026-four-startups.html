India's AI startups raised $1.6 billion. Four firms took 72%

#IndiaAI#AIFunding#Startups#AIInfrastructure

TL;DR

  • Indian AI startups raised $1.6 billion across 81 rounds in January to September 2026, up from $677 million a year earlier.
  • But the number of rounds fell 43 per cent and the number of companies that raised money dropped 44 per cent.
  • Four deals alone, led by Neysa's $600 million round, accounted for 72 per cent of all the money.
  • Startups building AI applications raised 27 per cent less than last year, as investors pushed for paying customers.

01.The headline number, and the number behind it

Indian artificial intelligence startups raised $1.6 billion across 81 funding rounds in the first nine months of 2026, compared with $677 million in the same period a year earlier. That is more than double the money in a single year, and on its own it looks like a boom. The figures come from Tracxn data shared with ET AI.

The second number is the one that changes the story. The count of funding rounds fell 43 per cent from 142 to 81, and the number of companies that actually raised money dropped 44 per cent, from 126 to 70. So the pot got bigger while fewer hands reached into it. Four transactions alone made up 72 per cent of all the money: Neysa's $600 million round, Sarvam's $255 million round, Yotta's $150 million and app-building platform Emergent's $130 million. Neha Singh, co-founder of Tracxn, put it plainly: funding has risen in dollars but not in the number of deals, and almost all of the rise comes from infrastructure rounds.

02.Where the money actually went

AI infrastructure took $1.2 billion across 19 rounds in the nine months, which is 75 per cent of all Indian AI funding. That is 8.3 times the $145 million raised a year earlier, even though the number of rounds fell from 29. Neysa and Yotta together raised $750 million for AI cloud and computing. Sarvam raised $346 million across two rounds, $255 million in June and $90 million in August, to build foundational models.

The cheque-writers have changed too. Private equity firms, technology companies and strategic investors are now putting money into AI infrastructure alongside traditional venture capital. Blackstone led Neysa's round. Sarvam's funding included HCL Technologies and Nvidia. Yotta's round included the Serum Institute of India. Karan Kirpalani, chief product officer at Neysa, said building, interconnecting, cooling and operating high-density clusters reliably needs specialised skills across power, cooling, networking and distributed systems, and that engineering capability has to expand as deployments scale.

03.The squeeze at the application layer

Companies building AI applications went the other way. They raised $390 million across 62 rounds, down 27 per cent from $532 million across 113 rounds a year earlier. Vertical AI, which serves specific industries, fell 87 per cent to $41 million from $319 million. Consumer AI applications attracted just $1 million across three rounds. Enterprise AI was the exception, pulling in $348 million, up 70 per cent, though two companies took most of that money.

Investors are asking harder questions. Venkat Vallabhaneni, managing partner at Inflexor Ventures, said most of what gets pitched to him is still built on models trained elsewhere, and a good share of it is a thin layer on top. He also described the customer problem in one line: Indian enterprises love to pilot, they are much slower to sign a real purchase order, and a startup can lose a year in pilots that go nowhere. Shweta Rajpal Kohli, president and CEO of the Startup Policy Forum, said startups still struggle with affordable computing, quality datasets, specialised research talent and long-term investors, and that getting businesses to adopt and pay for products remains a separate battle.

04.India against the rest of the world

The global picture is the same pattern at a much larger scale. AI companies raised $519 billion worldwide up to mid-September 2026, according to the State of AI Report 2026 by Air Street Capital and Dealroom. The United States accounted for $393 billion, or 76 per cent of the total, followed by China with $59 billion and Europe with $40 billion. OpenAI and Anthropic together raised $217 billion, about 42 per cent of all global AI funding.

India's $1.6 billion is a small slice of that global pot. That is worth holding on to before reading too much into the year-on-year jump. India is not competing at the frontier of the largest model-building budgets, and the data does not pretend otherwise. What the numbers do show is a country building the plumbing, compute and cloud capacity that Indian companies will rent, while the application layer above it is still thin, crowded and short of paying customers.

05.What this means if you are in India

For students and working professionals, the hiring signal is fairly clear. The money is flowing into data centres, GPU clusters and model training, which means demand for people who can run power, cooling, networking and distributed systems, not only people who can prompt a model. Enterprise AI is the other pocket still growing, and it rewards engineers who understand a customer's existing software well enough to plug into it. Krishna Khandelwal, co-founder and CEO of conversational AI startup Hunar.AI, said enterprises increasingly want AI that can execute complete workflows and fit into the systems they already run.

For founders and small businesses, the lesson is less about raising money and more about what the money is buying. Vallabhaneni said startups can build stronger businesses through data they own, specialised engineering knowledge, regulatory approvals and products that work closely with a customer's existing systems. Ankit Kedia of Capital-A sees room in manufacturing, industrial automation, robotics, energy and supply chains, where AI improves productivity and cuts operating costs in ways a buyer can measure. Kohli's summary is the one to keep: the most valuable AI businesses will be the ones that understand a particular industry, solve a problem better than anyone else, and build something customers are willing to pay for.

How much did Indian AI startups raise in 2026?

Which Indian AI companies raised the biggest rounds?

Why are AI application startups finding it harder?

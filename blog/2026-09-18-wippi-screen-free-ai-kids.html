Wippi: India's Screen-Free AI Companion for Kids

#Wippi#KidsTech#ScreenFree#IndianStartups

TL;DR

  • Indian startup Wippi has raised $1.2 million in seed funding for a screen-free AI companion for children
  • It combines proprietary hardware with conversational AI, character cards and interactive voice-first storytelling
  • Its story universe runs from Cinderella to the Ramayana — kids listen and talk instead of watching a screen
  • Wippi is targeting more than 15,000 families over the next 12 months; a whole anti-doomscrolling startup wave is building behind it

The most interesting AI startup story in India this week isn't another chatbot or coding agent. It's a company betting that parents will happily pay for AI that takes their kids away from screens. Wippi, an Indian startup building a screen-free AI companion for children, has just raised $1.2 million in seed funding — and it's part of a small but fast-growing group of Indian founders who believe the next big AI business might be the one that helps people put the phone down.

01.The startup that wants kids off screens

Wippi was founded in 2024 by Rishabh Singhi and Siddhartha Jain, who have known each other for more than two decades. The founding insight is deeply relatable to any Indian parent: Singhi's experience as a new father is what set him searching for alternatives to screens for young children. His conclusion — "if we were to take kids off the screen, we have to give them something that is even more interesting" — is basically the whole product strategy in one line.

The startup's framing is smart. It isn't anti-technology. Wippi takes AI off the screen: children interact with characters through voice, not video. No autoplay, no infinite feed, no recommendation engine quietly optimising for their attention. Just a conversation.

02.How the screen-free AI companion works

Wippi combines proprietary hardware with conversational AI, character cards and interactive storytelling. Instead of watching characters on a screen, children talk to them. The fictional universe spans familiar stories and characters ranging from Cinderella to the Ramayana — a nice touch for Indian households where grandma's stories and bedtime tales still rule.

What I like about the design is that it asks children to participate rather than just consume. A kid who is arguing with a character about what happens next in a story is doing something very different from a kid glassy-eyed in front of yet another cartoon compilation. "Scrolling as a concept still exists," as one founder in this space put it. "It becomes an issue when it's endless."

03.The $1.2 million bet

Wippi's seed round was led by 12 Flags, with participation from Warmup Ventures, Ventana Ventures and Ruvento Ventures — the company's first institutional funding. The plan for the money is refreshingly concrete: develop the voice-AI technology, expand the engineering and AI teams, scale manufacturing in India, and grow the direct-to-consumer business. The target is more than 15,000 families over the next 12 months.

Honestly, what surprised me most is how small that number feels against the problem. There are tens of millions of Indian families with young children and a doomscrolling problem that starts earlier every year. If Wippi's hardware-plus-subscription model works even modestly, the ceiling here is enormous — and it's hardware built and manufactured in India, which fits neatly into the broader domestic AI manufacturing push we've been tracking.

04.Fullhouse and the anti-scroll wave

Wippi isn't alone. Fullhouse, another Indian startup, is making the same bet for a very different audience — people in their 60s and 70s. Each morning, users get 13 cards in the app: a quiz, some trivia, a task, a short challenge. After the 13th card, the experience ends for the day. On purpose. One of its most popular features is a quiz modelled on Kaun Banega Crorepati, and users have sent in screenshots of scores running into lakhs.

The early numbers are telling. Fullhouse says retention among users who make it past the first three days is above 80 percent, with more than 2,000 users tested so far. It's bootstrapped, with incubation support from Antler and backing from IIM Bangalore's NSRCEL. The startup's biggest hurdle isn't engagement — it's trust, because older Indians have either faced online fraud or been warned about it by their families.

05.Why this matters for Indian families

Every parent I know is fighting some version of the same battle: the tablet at dinner, the YouTube binge before bed, the tantrum when the screen goes off. Indian households have largely solved it with guilt and willpower, because the market never offered a real alternative. For the first time, AI is making the alternative possible — an experience engaging enough to compete with a feed, without being a feed.

Pentagon Probe: AI Overreliance and the Minab School Strike

#Palantir#MavenAI#MilitaryAI#Pentagon#AISafety

TL;DR

  • A Pentagon investigation found flawed intelligence, outdated imagery and overreliance on AI contributed to a February missile strike on a school in Minab, Iran, that killed 123 children
  • US Central Command personnel leaned heavily on Palantir's Maven Smart System, expecting it to catch stale or inconsistent intelligence — something it was never designed to do
  • A 2019 warning about the site sat in a database disconnected from the targeting system
  • The core lesson extends far beyond the military: AI is only as reliable as the data and the humans behind it

Some AI news is exciting, some is odd, and then there are stories that stop you in your tracks. This week, a Bloomberg investigation into a Pentagon review gave us the hardest kind of AI story there is — what happens when overreliance on AI in a military targeting process meets data that was simply wrong. On February 28, during the opening days of the US–Iran war, two Tomahawk missiles struck the Shajarah Tayyebeh school in the southern Iranian town of Minab. More than 150 people were killed, including at least 123 children. The Pentagon's own investigators have now pieced together how it happened, and AI is central to the answer.

01.What the Pentagon investigation found

According to Bloomberg, which spoke with officials involved in the internal review, three things combined to produce the disaster. First, the administration had demanded an overwhelming aerial assault — more than 1,000 Iranian targets were struck within the first 24 hours — and that pace compressed the time available to verify each target. Second, the information about the site itself was outdated. The location had once been part of a military compound, but years earlier it had been physically separated by walls and had operated openly as a school. The satellite imagery showed the changes. The targeting database did not. Third, cuts to civilian-protection personnel meant that nobody on the remaining teams reviewed the strike plans before the missiles flew.

Outdated intelligence. Rushed timelines. Missing human checks. Any one of those alone is dangerous. Together, they were fatal.

02.Where Palantir's Maven comes in

Maven Smart System, built by Palantir, is an AI-enabled platform that pulls together intelligence from many data sources to help commanders identify potential targets and make decisions. It has become, in Bloomberg's words, a cornerstone of the US military apparatus over the past year. The problem, investigators found, was not that Maven malfunctioned. It's that some US Central Command personnel came to rely on it so heavily that they expected it to flag stale or inconsistent intelligence — a job the system was never built or trained to do. The AI was working exactly as designed. The design assumed the data flowing into it was current and correct. It wasn't.

Palantir, for its part, says it is "not responsible for the underlying data nor identifying intelligence deficiencies," and that there's no evidence its software was at fault. According to the reporting, the company has since built new tools to re-review underlying intelligence and flag inconsistencies that human review might miss.

03.The warning that never arrived

Here's the detail that honestly bothered me the most. A US intelligence analyst had reportedly noticed changes to the site as far back as 2019. But that warning was entered into a system that was not connected to the authoritative targeting database. So the information existed, inside the same government, and still never reached the people preparing the strike. This wasn't an AI failure in the narrow sense — it was an information-flow failure that an overworked, AI-accelerated process never caught. The Pentagon reportedly knew within hours that US missiles were responsible.

There's also a broader legal shadow over all of this. A UN-backed fact-finding mission said on Thursday that there are reasonable grounds to believe the US committed war crimes in two February airstrikes in Iran, one of them the Minab school strike. That's an allegation from a UN body, not a court verdict — but it guarantees this story is far from over.

04.The uncomfortable lesson for everyone who uses AI

Here's the thing: most of us will never touch a military targeting system. But the failure pattern here is universal, and every company in India racing to adopt AI should study it. US defence AI oversight is itself being rebuilt around the same time — the new 'AI Force' czar plan we covered is meant to be part of the answer. An AI system does not know when its inputs are stale. It does not know that the building on the map changed purpose years ago. It will confidently process outdated information and present the output with the same polish as accurate information — which is precisely when humans stop checking. Speed without verification isn't efficiency. It's risk, scaled up.

That's why the boring parts of AI — data hygiene, human review checkpoints, knowing what a system can and cannot do — deserve as much attention as the flashy parts.

Delhi's Seva-First Innovation Challenge 2026: ₹2 Crore for Young Innovators

TL;DR

  • Delhi's Seva-First Innovation Challenge 2026 launched at DTU with a ₹2 crore prize pool
  • A 100-day challenge open to universities, schools, ITIs, polytechnics, startups and industry across the entire northern region
  • Mentorship from Viraj Bahl (Veeba), Akash Gupta (Zypp), Swati Bhargava (CashKaro) and Puneet Gupta (AstroTalk)
  • DTU will also send engineering students to villages from the 2027-28 session under a new rural innovation programme

Here's something for every student reading this on a hostel Wi-Fi connection. On Saturday, Delhi's Higher Education Minister Ashish Sood launched the Seva-First Innovation Challenge 2026 at Delhi Technological University — a 100-day sprint with a ₹2 crore prize pool, structured mentorship and government support for young people building technology that solves real problems.

01.What the Seva-First Challenge actually is

The idea, as Sood put it at the launch, is simple: "we want to serve society by finding solutions to real-world problems." Teams will identify national and local challenges, generate ideas, develop prototypes, get them validated, and then work towards actually implementing solutions. That last stage is the unusual part — most student competitions end at the demo; this one is designed to carry good ideas towards deployment.

DTU is the anchor university, and the challenge covers the whole northern region — Haryana, Punjab, Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Chandigarh and Ladakh. So this isn't just a Delhi event with a fancy name; it's regional by design.

02.Who can participate — and this is the good part

Universities, industry, startups, schools, ITIs and polytechnics across these states and Union Territories can all take part. I want to underline that: ITIs and polytechnics. Most innovation challenges in India quietly become a playground for the top engineering colleges. Bringing industrial training institutes into the same arena as IIT hopefuls and funded startups is a genuinely smart touch — a lot of India's real-world problem-solving talent sits in those classrooms.

03.The mentors are the real prize

Honestly, what caught my eye more than the ₹2 crore is the mentor list. Startup leaders and unicorn founders signed up to guide participants — Viraj Bahl of Veeba, Akash Gupta of Zypp, Swati Bhargava of CashKaro and Puneet Gupta of AstroTalk. For a student in, say, Dehradun or Jalandhar, getting direct feedback from founders who've built consumer brands and scaled them past a billion dollars is worth more than any prize cheque. That's the kind of access you normally can't buy.

04.The bigger picture Sood was painting

The minister framed the challenge against Delhi's startup ambitions — under the Delhi Startup Policy, the government aims to create 10,000 startups and 4 lakh jobs in the capital. He also pointed out how far the ecosystem has travelled: from fewer than 500 registered startups before 2014 to the world's third-largest startup ecosystem today. And there's a Viksit Bharat 2047 angle — Sood was blunt that it "cannot remain only a government initiative" and must become a people's movement.

One more thing worth knowing: DTU announced the Rural Technology and Community Engagement Programme (RTCEP) at the same event, starting from the 2027-28 academic session. Engineering students will go to villages to find local challenges and build practical fixes. Sood's pitch for it was my favourite line of the day: "If a farmer around you is struggling, don't just notice it; solve it."

05.Why this matters if you're a student or founder

Put this alongside what else is happening for young builders right now — the ₹1 lakh crore RDI scheme launching in November, and the wave of AI skilling programmes we've covered from IIT Kanpur's teacher training onwards. The doors for a 19-year-old with a laptop and an idea have never been this wide open in India.

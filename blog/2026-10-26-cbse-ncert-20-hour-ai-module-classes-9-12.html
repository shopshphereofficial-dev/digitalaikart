CBSE Puts AI in the Classroom: 20-Hour Module for Classes 9-12

#AI#AIIndia#CBSE#Education

TL;DR

  • NCERT has released a 20-hour introductory module on computational thinking and AI for students in Classes 9 to 12.
  • It covers everyday AI applications, how machines learn from examples, deepfake detection and data privacy, and can be taught with or without digital devices.
  • CBSE has earmarked the module for school-based assessment in 2026-27, and it is compulsory for Class 9 under the board's revised scheme.
  • Timing, teacher training and assessment details are still to be published.

01.What NCERT has released

NCERT has released a 20-hour introductory module on computational thinking and artificial intelligence for students in Classes 9 to 12. PTI reported the release on 25 October 2026, and The Hindu carried the details. It is written for students with no prior knowledge of AI. NCERT says the aim is to help them understand how the technology works, judge its limitations, and become careful users who check what an AI system tells them instead of trusting the output.

The module was developed in consultation with IIT Madras and IIT Jodhpur. The topics are practical rather than mathematical: how AI shows up in everyday applications, how machines learn from examples, how to spot a deepfake, and where the ethical boundaries lie when personal data is involved. That is a deliberately different starting point from a coding course. The student is being taught to interrogate a system, not to build one.

02.Built for schools that may not have computers

NCERT describes the module as activity-based, built on discussions, practical exercises, experimentation and reflection rather than lectures. The detail that matters most for India is that the activities can be run with or without digital devices. A government school with a single shared laptop can teach the same module as a private school with a full computer lab, which is not true of most technology curricula.

The stated emphasis is on safe and ethical use: data privacy, misinformation, fairness, digital safety and critical verification of AI-generated information. In its release, NCERT said the module encourages students to become informed, confident and responsible technology users, while reinforcing that human judgement, creativity and ethical responsibility remain central to learning. It also says the module is meant to produce not just users of AI systems but evaluators of them.

03.How CBSE will use it

The module now has a place in assessment. In a circular dated 9 April, CBSE said NCERT would provide modules for Classes 9 to 12 for internal assessment during the 2026-27 academic session, The Times of India reported. The board has earmarked the new module for school-based assessment. Under its revised scheme, the computational thinking and AI modules are compulsory for Class 9, while Class 10 continues under the existing scheme.

Separate AI textbooks are also being prepared for students who take the subject at a more advanced level, so the 20-hour module works as a first step rather than the whole course. A senior education ministry official told the paper that AI had become part of everyday activity, from education and research to content writing and career guidance, and that students would be taught how to use AI tools correctly and to fact-check information.

04.Why it matters for students, parents and teachers

Most students in Classes 9 to 12 are already using AI tools, often without any guidance beyond a friend's account or a forwarded prompt. What has been missing is a shared baseline: what these systems are doing, where they go wrong, and what should never be pasted into one. A module that covers deepfakes and data privacy gives a 15-year-old a framework for the chatbot answer sitting in their homework, and gives parents a version of the conversation that does not depend on them being technical.

For teachers, the change is larger than 20 hours suggests. Running an activity about how a model learns from examples asks for a different kind of preparation than teaching a syllabus, and many teachers will meet the material at the same time as their students. The module's design, which needs no devices, lowers the equipment barrier. It does not lower the training barrier, and that is where the rollout will be judged.

05.What is still not clear

Several practical questions remain open. The announcements do not say when schools should begin teaching the module, how the 20 hours should be spread across an academic year, or how teachers will be trained to deliver it. Gadgets Now noted that implementation details, including assessments and teacher training, remain undefined. For a board that assesses through internal and school-based work, the guidance on what counts as evidence of learning will decide how seriously the module is taken.

The gap between schools is the other unknown. A curriculum that works without devices removes one obstacle, but not the difference in teacher time, class size and continuity between a well-resourced urban school and a small-town one. The safest reading of this week's news is that it is a real shift in what schools are asked to teach, and a good one, still waiting on the paperwork that turns a released module into a taught one.

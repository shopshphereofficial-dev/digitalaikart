Sanskrit Meets AI: Chennai's New 'Laya' Model Checks Every Answer Against Panini's Grammar

TL;DR

  • Madras Sanskrit College and US-based Articul8 AI launched the Sanskrit Heritage Model and Platform in Chennai on September 15, with Union Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman doing the honours
  • The family of models, called Laya, doesn't just predict the next word — it verifies every response against Panini's 2,500-year-old Ashtadhyayi grammar, encoded as a live compiler inside the system
  • A team of 10–15 Sanskrit scholars from the college is fine-tuning and validating its answers
  • It goes first to select institutions, with public releases planned in the coming weeks

Most AI news this week was about safety talks, funding rounds and chatbots getting faster. But the story that made me sit up came out of Mylapore, Chennai — a 120-year-old Sanskrit college teaming up with an American AI startup to build something that genuinely hasn't been tried before. And the finance minister showed up to launch it.

01.What actually launched

On Tuesday, the Madras Sanskrit College (MSC) and Articul8 AI launched the Sanskrit Heritage Model and Platform — a family of AI models called Laya, trained on a large volume of Sanskrit books and data from MSC's own collection alongside other publicly available documents. Union Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman launched it at an event in Chennai, and the college says this makes it the first institution in the country with an AI-powered knowledge model of this kind.

The bigger ambition is a whole platform: MSC wants to become what it calls the first virtual Sanskrit university globally, with AI-powered, personalised courses tailored to each learner's proficiency level.

02.The tech bit: a compiler, not autocomplete

Here's the part that separates Laya from every other language model out there. Normal AI models predict the next word based on statistical patterns in text. Laya does that too, but then runs what its makers call a "correctness stack" — it checks its own responses against the actual grammatical rules of Sanskrit before showing you anything.

How? They encoded Panini's Ashtadhyayi — the roughly 2,500-year-old grammar that defines how every correct Sanskrit word is formed — as a live compiler and verifier inside the system. As Arun K. Subramaniyan, founder and CEO of Articul8 AI, put it: "Sanskrit has a formal grammar that specifies, rule by rule, how every correct word is built. That means it is a language you can compute. We encoded Panini's Ashtadhyayi... as a live compiler and verifier inside the system, so every response is generated and verified by the same machinery before it reaches the scholar."

Honestly, what surprised me most was this detail. We've all seen AI confidently mess up Hindi verb endings or mangle Tamil transliteration. The idea of a model that mathematically cannot break grammar rules — because the grammar is baked into the generation pipeline itself — is a genuinely different approach to the hallucination problem.

03.Humans still in the loop

Don't picture a server room running unsupervised. Right now, a team of 10–15 scholars from MSC is testing, interpreting and validating the model's responses. The first release goes out to a select group of institutions, and a series of public releases is planned over the coming weeks. The approach is also designed to extend beyond Sanskrit to other Indic languages and knowledge systems where formal rules and source material matter.

04.What the Finance Minister said

Sitharaman made an interesting argument at the launch — that Sanskrit's precision and structured grammar give it a natural advantage in the AI era, since scholars building the language 3,000–4,000 years ago "knew to arrive at precision." She said AI should be used to help ordinary people navigate great works like those of poet Kalidasa, and even suggested AI could help trace how far Indian languages, including Tamil and Sanskrit, spread — using evidence from manuscripts, palm leaves and copper plates.

Her framing was forward-looking, not nostalgic: the goal shouldn't be restoring Sanskrit to some "old glory," but anchoring its knowledge for future generations. She also urged Articul8 to document Sanskrit's influence beyond India's borders.

05.Why this matters beyond Sanskrit

Think about what this means for Indian-language AI more broadly. India's strength so far has been scale — huge datasets, cheap engineering, big user bases. What it has struggled with is depth: models that actually respect the structure of our languages instead of treating them like translated English. A rule-verified approach like Laya's, if it extends to Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and the rest, could set a template for serious Indic-language AI — the kind universities, courts and archives could actually rely on.

It's also a reminder that the interesting opportunities in Indian AI aren't only in Bengaluru chatbot startups. Language technology, education, heritage, public services — that's a wide-open field where knowing how to direct these models matters more than a computer science degree.

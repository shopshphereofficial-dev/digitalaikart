One Million Teachers: Inside IIT Madras's AI Classroom Push

#AIIndia#AIEducation#IITMadras#TeacherTraining

TL;DR

  • Bodhan AI, an IIT Madras-incubated Centre of Excellence in AI for Education, said on 17 November it will train more than one million schoolteachers in AI literacy over the next 12 to 18 months.
  • The three-phase programme covers responsible AI use, practical classroom applications and school-level integration, with a train-the-trainer model running through state and district lead teachers.
  • It is one of several tracks: Andhra Pradesh has an AI tutor pilot with Bodhan AI, the Ministry of Education has a ChatGPT partnership for government school teachers, and Assam is training about one lakh teachers by May 2027.
  • Critics writing in The Hindu the same week argue AI is entering Indian schools through procurement deals rather than public debate, and ask who controls the tools and the children's learning data.

01.What IIT Madras announced this week

Bodhan AI, an artificial intelligence initiative incubated at IIT Madras, said on 17 November that it has tied up with three education organisations to take AI literacy and classroom integration to schoolteachers across India. Its stated target is more than one million educators over the next 12 to 18 months, DT Next reported.

The division of work is specific. Learning Links Foundation and Vidya Shakti will support implementation nationwide, while the Centre for Teacher Accreditation, known as CENTA, will facilitate the rollout in Andhra Pradesh. The Hindu carried the announcement on 18 November. On its own site, Bodhan AI says it is supported by the Ministry of Education and is building the Bharat EduAI Stack, which it describes as a sovereign digital public infrastructure for learning and teaching.

Karthik Mahadevan Mohanakrishnan, chief operating officer at Bodhan AI, told DT Next the initiative is also being pursued with IIT Madras's Wadhwani School of Data Science and AI, the National Council of Educational Research and Training, and several state governments.

02.Three phases, and what a teacher actually learns

The programme runs in three phases: AI literacy and responsible use, practical applications in teaching, and school-level integration. Teachers get hands-on training in lesson planning, content creation, assessment, evaluation and personalised feedback. They are also taught to spot AI-generated inaccuracies, bias and data privacy risks, which is the part that matters most in a classroom.

Delivery follows a train-the-trainer model. Master trainers prepare lead teachers at state and district level, and those lead teachers then take the material into individual schools. That structure is how a target of a million teachers becomes feasible without a million separate workshops.

There is an early signal of what it can do. P Nagarajan, project director at Vidya Shakti, said the organisation has already trained around 4,000 teachers across six states and one Union Territory, and that those teachers reported the AI assistant saved them at least 30 minutes a day on repetitive tasks. That figure comes from a partner describing its own programme, not from an independent evaluation, so treat it as a claim to test rather than a result to bank.

03.It is one track of several, not the whole plan

The Bodhan programme sits inside a wider push. The Hindu notes that the Ministry of Education has partnered with OpenAI to give ChatGPT to government school teachers from Classes 1 to 12, and that Andhra Pradesh has announced an AI tutor pilot with Bodhan AI in its government schools. In Assam, Samagra Shiksha agreed with IIT Madras and Bodhan AI to train around one lakh teachers in Grades 6 to 12 by 31 May 2027, in Assamese and Bodo, the Times of India reported on 16 November.

The same week brought a second skilling announcement, this one for college students. Gnani AI, a startup selected under the India AI Mission, signed a memorandum of understanding with Karnataka's Department of Electronics, IT and Biotechnology at the Bangalore Tech Summit 2026 to train 1,000 engineering and science students in voice AI and agentic AI, Hindustan Times reported on 18 November. The modules include multilingual speech recognition, text-to-speech, language models, prompt engineering, API integration, retrieval-augmented generation and multi-agent systems, with graded labs and capstone projects.

Gnani will give students access to its models, platforms and computing resources, plus mentorship from its engineers. Its Inya VoiceOS, a five-billion-parameter voice-to-voice model, was unveiled by Prime Minister Narendra Modi at the India AI Impact Summit in February.

04.The argument against moving this fast

Not everyone reads the speed as progress. In The Hindu on 18 November, Jaya Ramchandani argued that AI is arriving in Indian schools through procurement deals rather than public debate. A school system built on a foreign company's tools, she wrote, can be repriced, changed or switched off by someone else, and children's learning data becomes an asset to be sold. She pointed to New York City's September 2026 moratorium on generative AI for nearly 600,000 public school students up to Class 8, and its ban on companion chatbots in every grade.

It is worth separating two things that get blurred in this argument. Training a teacher to use AI for lesson plans and marking is not the same as putting a chatbot in front of a child, and the Bodhan programme is teacher-facing. But the harder questions still apply: which tools are used, where the data goes, what the contract says at renewal, and whether a school can walk away without losing its working material.

05.What teachers, parents and school leaders should do now

For teachers, the practical advice is to start with one repetitive task, such as drafting a worksheet or writing comment banks for report cards, and to check every output before it reaches a student. The programme's own emphasis on spotting inaccuracies, bias and privacy risk is the most useful part of the syllabus, because an assistant that quietly invents a date or a formula is worse than none.

For parents, the question to ask the school is concrete: which AI tools are in use, what student data do they hold, and does a teacher review AI output before a child sees it. For school leaders and administrators, ask about exit terms and data ownership before signing, and insist that any pilot can be switched off cheaply. A tool you cannot leave is not a pilot.

For students and fresh graduates, the Karnataka syllabus is a fair reading list of what employers are hiring for right now: speech processing, retrieval-augmented generation, and agents that can complete a task across several steps. None of it requires a costly degree to begin.

Frequently asked questions

How many teachers will the Bodhan AI programme train?
Bodhan AI said on 17 November 2026 that the target is more than one million schoolteachers over the next 12 to 18 months, delivered with Learning Links Foundation and Vidya Shakti nationally and CENTA in Andhra Pradesh. The programme is teacher-facing and runs in three phases, ending with school-level integration.

Which states are covered so far?
Andhra Pradesh is named for rollout support through CENTA, and Assam has a separate agreement with Samagra Shiksha and IIT Madras to train around one lakh teachers in Grades 6 to 12 by 31 May 2027 in Assamese and Bodo. Bodhan AI says it is working with several state governments, but reports have not listed all of them, and no enrolment link for individual teachers has been published.

Does this mean AI will replace teachers?
No. The programme positions AI as an assistant for lesson planning, content creation, assessment and feedback, with teachers deciding what reaches students. Its syllabus explicitly includes identifying AI-generated inaccuracies, bias and data privacy risks, which assumes a teacher is checking the output rather than trusting it.

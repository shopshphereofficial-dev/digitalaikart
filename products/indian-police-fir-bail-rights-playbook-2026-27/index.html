What the law lets you do — in order, from the police counter to the bail order.

Frequently Asked Questions

Everything you need to know before buying

Is this legal advice?

No. It is legal information: the statute, the judgments, the procedures and the drafts, set out in order so that you can understand the system and ask better questions. It does not create a lawyer-client relationship. For anything that could end in an arrest or a charge, speak to an advocate — and if you cannot afford one, Chapter 16 tells you how to get one free, which is an entitlement under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987.

Which law does the book follow — the old CrPC or the new BNSS?

The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, in force since 1 July 2024. Every provision is given in its current BNSS number, with a full CrPC-to-BNSS mapping table so that you can translate advice written before 2024. That table alone saves you from quoting section 154 at a counter in 2026.

What is the January 2026 Supreme Court ruling the book covers?

In Satender Kumar Antil v. Central Bureau of Investigation, an order dated 15 January 2026 reported as 2026 INSC 115, the Supreme Court held that a notice under section 35(3) of the BNSS is mandatory for offences punishable with imprisonment up to seven years, and that arrest is the exception rather than the rule. Chapter 9 sets out the ruling and the exact paragraph to say if you or someone you know is facing arrest.

Does the book work in my state?

The statutes are central, so sections 173, 175, 35, 47, 58, 187, 479, 482 and 396 apply everywhere. Where something is state-specific — legal aid income ceilings, victim compensation amounts, police complaints authorities, e-FIR portals — the book says so plainly and points you to the official source instead of printing a number that would be wrong in your state.

I have already been refused an FIR. Is it too late?

No. Chapter 7 gives you the full escalation ladder with dates: a written complaint with a receipt, a speed-post letter to the Superintendent of Police under section 173(4), a follow-up, and then an application to a magistrate under section 175(3) supported by an affidavit. Chapter 8 adds the three questions that close a preliminary enquiry under section 173(3), which has a fourteen-day statutory limit.

Who is this playbook for?

Electronics, electrical and instrumentation students and graduates; diploma and ITI holders looking for technician and equipment roles; B.Sc and M.Sc physics or materials graduates who want fab and reliability work; and working IT professionals trying to move into DFT, physical design or test engineering. Chapter 5 maps five reader profiles to five routes.

Does it promise a job or a placement?

No, and it says so on page two. No PDF can get anyone hired. What it does is shorten the distance between where you are and the interview: which role family to target, what to learn in what order, what to build, how to write the resume, and how to reach people who can refer you.

Is the information current?

Every plant status, scheme detail, fee and date was checked against primary sources in the last week of October and the first week of November 2026 — PIB releases, India Semiconductor Mission documents, MeitY and NIELIT pages, the GATE 2027 information bulletin and company announcements. Chapter 25 lists every source. Salary bands come from jobs-platform data and are labelled as market ranges, not official statistics.

Do I need to buy software or a paid course to follow the study plans?

No. Every tool in Chapter 15 is free and open source — Icarus Verilog, Verilator, GTKWave, Yosys, OpenSTA, OpenROAD, the SkyWater 130nm PDK, ngspice and KLayout. The only paid option the book mentions is NIELIT's government-run VLSI programme at Rs. 40,000 for thirty weeks, and it is optional.

I am from a tier-3 college with average marks. Is this still useful?

That is exactly who several chapters are written for. Chapter 10 builds the case for verification as the most accessible design role, Chapter 12 covers the technician route into packaging and fab plants, and Chapter 17 is a full referral system because off-campus hiring in this industry runs on people rather than portals.
